Think you don't have time to write stories? Try closing each day with a single sentence. One sentence takes less than a minute, yet after a few weeks you hold a small pile of real moments, and any one of them can grow into a story when you are ready.

## Why one sentence works

A blank page asks a lot of you: a topic, a shape, a mood. A single sentence asks almost nothing, so you actually do it. Because you write it the same evening, the details are still close. And because the sentence is small, there is no pressure to make it good. You are not writing a story yet. You are saving the seed of one.

## Three steps for a daily sentence

1. **Pick a fixed moment.** Right after brushing your teeth, on the train home, or while the kettle heats up. Tie the habit to something you already do every day.
2. **Write where you were and what shifted.** Name the place, then one small change: a plan that fell through, a sound that stopped, a conversation that took a different turn. Keep it to one sentence, even if it runs long.
3. **Stop there.** Don't explain, don't add a lesson, don't fix the grammar. The sentence is allowed to be rough. When you feel the urge to write more, save that urge for the weekend.

## Example: one week of sentences

A week of entries might look like this:

> Monday: In the stairwell at work, the light timer clicked off while I was mid-sentence on the phone, and I finished the call in the dark.
>
> Wednesday: At the bakery counter, the person ahead of me ordered the last roll, then turned around and split it in half.
>
> Friday: On the bus home I missed my stop on purpose and walked the extra twenty minutes.

None of these is a finished story. But each one has a place, a small turn and a detail you could build on. On the weekend, reread the week and circle the sentence that still tugs at you. That one is your next story.

## Common stumbling blocks

- **Writing about feelings only.** "I was tired and a bit sad" gives you nothing to build on later. Swap in what you could see or hear, such as the light timer or the half roll.
- **Turning it into a long entry.** Once it grows into a page, the habit gets heavy and you skip days. If you want to write more, do it after the sentence, and count the sentence as the win.
- **Skipping a day and quitting.** A missed day is just a gap. Write one sentence the next evening and carry on. The pile doesn't have to be unbroken to be useful.

## Tips for using draft()

draft() takes about thirty seconds of what you say, adds two or three gentle questions, and shapes your answers into a story with a hook, rising action, a peak, a reflection and a close. When you pick a sentence from your week, say it out loud as the start of your thirty seconds. The questions that follow often ask about the small turn you already noted.

## Try this today

Tonight, write one sentence: where you were, and one thing that shifted. Don't polish it. Tomorrow evening, write another. At the end of the week, read them in order and circle the one you would like to hear more about.
