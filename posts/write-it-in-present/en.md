Tell the story of nearly taking the wrong bag off a train and you might give away the mix-up in the first sentence. You already know how it ends. At the time, you heard the station name, saw the gray bag on the rack and reached for it. Only then did you notice its handle had no blue ribbon. Write those seconds in the present and the reader can notice the missing ribbon almost when you do.

## What changes when you write in the present

Writing in the present can show you when a sentence knows too much too soon. “I reach for someone else's bag” gives away what you find out as you reach. Break it into two sentences and give what you see or hear at each moment its own sentence. Let the order of discovery guide the order of your sentences.

## Three steps to put one scene in the present

1. **Choose a moment with its beginning and end close together.** Start when you hear the station name and look up at the rack. Stop when you see that the ribbon is missing.
2. **Write things in the order you notice them.** You hear the announcement, see the bag, and look at its handle. At each sentence, ask how much you know at that point.
3. **Check that the verbs stay in the same tense.** Read the verbs together: “I hear,” “I look up,” “I reach.” Those plain verbs are enough to keep the scene moving.

## The bag on the train rack

> I heard the station name. I looked up at the rack and reached for the gray bag. There was no blue ribbon on the handle.

Now try the same moment in the present.

> I hear the station name. I look up at the rack and reach for the gray bag. There is no blue ribbon on the handle.

Both versions show the same things in the same order. Read them aloud and see which one makes the reach and the glance for the ribbon feel clearer. If the rest of the piece is in the past, you can bring back just the order you found in the exercise.

## What to look for when you revise

After changing the verbs, look for lines that give away a later discovery. “I'm about to notice a mistake” tells the reader to expect one. Take it out and reread from the sight of the bag. Pass over anything you remember dimly and keep the details you remember in order.

## Tips for using draft()

After you speak for about thirty seconds, draft() offers two or three gentle questions. Slow down when a moment comes back clearly as you talk. Say what you noticed first in one sentence. You can return to it when you write.

## Try this today

Pick a few seconds from this week. Write three sentences in the order you remember things, then put the same sentences into the present tense and read them aloud. If you learn something in the last sentence, check whether an earlier sentence gives it away.
