When we write a story we usually write only the places where things happened: what took place at home, what took place at the office. The way between them gets skipped. Yet the short trip from one place to another is often where the day's feelings show most honestly. Add one line about the few minutes you spent walking or riding, and the story links together more naturally.

## Why the way between can link a story

On the way, a person is alone. They may turn over what just happened or worry ahead about what comes next. When there is room to breathe between scenes, the reader breathes with them and follows the change in feeling. A trip is also the easiest place to catch the season, the weather and the sounds of the street.

## Three steps to write the way between

1. **Write where you left and where you arrived, in one line.** Keep it simple: "on the way home from the clinic."
2. **Pick one thing you saw or heard on the way.** It can be as minor as a traffic light, a passing bicycle or a shop sign's glow.
3. **Add the thought in your head, in one sentence.** Say briefly whether you were turning over the last place or worrying about the next.

## Example: the bus home

Say this is what happened today:

> The meeting ran long. I got home and ate dinner.

Now write in the way between:

> I rested my forehead against the bus window. At each stop another light came on. The thing I hadn't said in the meeting repeated in my head, in the same order, for three stops.

Without the word "frustrated," the feeling comes through.

## Common stumbling blocks

- **Writing the way too long.** One or two sentences are enough. When it grows long, the in-between becomes the main character.
- **Writing only scenery.** Always tie what you saw to the thought you had. Scenery alone reads like a postcard.
- **Listing every step of the trip in order.** You don't need every transfer or step count. Pull up just one thing.

## Tips for using draft()

After you talk for about thirty seconds, draft() offers two or three gentle questions. While you speak, stop once and ask yourself, "Where did I go after that, and how?" When the way comes to mind, the link between the peak of the story and its ending often gets clearer along with it.

## Try this today

Pick one trip you made today. Write the start and the end in one line, and what you saw plus the thought you had in another. Two lines are enough. Read them aloud and check whether the scenes before and after connect naturally.
