Most scenes begin with what we saw. "It was a rainy evening and I was sitting in a café." That is the easiest opening, and often the flattest. Open the same scene with a single sound, smell or touch and the reader is inside it in one sentence.

## Why one sense is enough

Piling on color, light, music and smell at once makes the first line heavy. One sense, chosen well, tells the reader where they are, and they fill in the rest themselves.

It works this way in life too. When we walk into an unfamiliar room, we notice one thing first: a clock ticking, the smell of bread, the cold of a door handle. The rest comes later. If your first sentence follows that same order, the reader recognizes it and trusts you from the first words.

## Three ways to choose

1. **Sound: what could you hear with your eyes closed?** A spoon against a saucer, a distant train, slippers in a hallway.
2. **Smell: what reached you before you saw anything?** Wet pavement, toast, a borrowed coat.
3. **Touch: what did your skin notice first?** A cold railing, a warm mug, a sleeve that was too long.

Don't pick the sense that sounds most poetic. Pick the one you felt first in that moment.

If you are unsure, replay the scene in your head once more, slowly, like frames with the picture turned off. Ask yourself: what did I hear before I looked around? What did my skin feel before I raised my eyes? The first thing that surfaces is usually the most honest one. Even the most ordinary details work, because they ring true precisely when they are not invented.

## Example: a rainy café

> It was a rainy evening and I was sitting in a café.

Opened with one sense:

> The spoon kept clinking against the saucer. Outside, the rain had started to drum on the awning. I was still stirring coffee that was already mixed.

Nothing that was seen has been mentioned, yet the reader is already sitting in that café.

Try the same scene with smell: "The café smelled of a wet umbrella and warm bread." Or with touch: "The cup was so hot that I held it with both hands." Each version has its own character, but they work the same way: one detail first, and only then the rest of the world. Put the versions side by side and you will see which one is closest to what you really lived through.

## Common stumbling blocks

- **Stacking all five senses.** One is a hook; five is an inventory.
- **Choosing a sense for effect.** If you didn't really smell it, don't write it.
- **Stopping at the sense.** After the first line, move on to what happened.

## Tips for using draft()

When you talk for about thirty seconds about the moment and draft() asks its gentle questions, answer with what you heard, smelled or touched, not only what you saw. Those small answers are the raw material for your first line.

## Try this today

1. Think of a place you were in a moment ago.
2. Close your eyes and ask yourself what you could hear.
3. Write that sound as the first sentence of the scene.
4. Only then add, in a sentence or two, where you were and what was happening.

You don't need a perfect sentence on the first try. One line you want to keep going from is enough. Tomorrow, repeat the exercise with a smell or a touch and notice how the opening changes.
