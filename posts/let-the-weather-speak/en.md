The moment a character starts explaining how they feel, the scene stiffens. Instead of writing "she felt lonely" or "he was glad," write one line about the weather at that moment. Weather sets a mood faster than almost anything else without explaining itself, and the reader looks at the sky and works out the feeling alone.

## Why weather can stand in for a feeling

Everyone knows weather with the body. The damp of a rainy day, the sting of noon sun, the restlessness of a windy afternoon all reach us without any explanation. Because the weather fixes the temperature of the place, the character does not have to say what they feel, and you need fewer words to add it on top.

## Three steps to write a feeling through weather

1. **Pick the weather of the scene in one word.** Clear, fog, downpour, cold wind: whichever comes to mind first is fine.
2. **Write the moment that weather touches the character's body.** A specific spot works best: the cuff of a sleeve, the back of the neck, the inside of a shoe.
3. **Add one action showing how the character deals with it.** Do they fold the umbrella, close the window, or just stand there and get wet?

## Example: the day of the breakup

Say you first wrote this:

> After he spoke my heart collapsed. I couldn't say a word.

Now rewrite it with weather:

> A thin rain was falling outside. I could feel it seeping into my sleeve and still I didn't open my umbrella. Only after he had gone did I wring out the wet cuff once.

Without "my heart collapsed," the feeling is there to see.

## Common stumbling blocks

- **Always picking weather that matches the mood.** Rain in every sad scene gets predictable. Sadness on a bright day can stay with a reader longer.
- **Describing the weather at length.** One or two sentences are enough. When the sky becomes the main character, the person disappears.
- **Ending on scenery with no bodily reaction.** Always tie the weather to one place where it touches the character.

## Tips for using draft()

After you talk for about thirty seconds, draft() offers two or three gentle questions. While you speak, stop once and ask yourself, "What was the sky like then?" When the weather comes back to you, the mood between the start of the story and its peak often gets clearer as well.

## Try this today

Recall one kind of weather you met outside today. Write one line about where it touched your body and one line about what you did then. Two lines are enough. Read them aloud and check whether the feeling shows even though you never named it.
