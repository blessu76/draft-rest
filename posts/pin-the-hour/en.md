A story that opens with "one afternoon" blurs in the reader's mind almost at once. A story that opens with "the 6:40 bus went past the stop" feels like standing right there. A time of day is a tiny piece of information, but it fixes a scene firmly into one slot of the day. Glancing at a clock and writing down a single time can make a story much clearer.

## Why one time of day can bring a scene to life

A time brings light, sound and mood with it. Seven in the morning and eleven at night turn the same kitchen into two different places. A time also signals hurry or waiting. Once "five minutes left" appears, the reader starts to feel the pressure. You don't have to explain a feeling, because the time sets the mood for you.

## Three steps to pin the hour

1. **Pick one short moment.** Anything from today that lasted a few minutes is enough: putting on shoes at the door, answering a phone call.
2. **Write the time in one line.** If you don't remember the exact number, use a nearby time or the state of the sky. For example: "Around seven, when the streetlights were still on."
3. **Add what that time did.** Say in one sentence whether the time made you hurry, wait or stop. This is the step that makes the time do something inside the story.

## Example: the bus stop

Say this is what happened today:

> I waited for the bus at the stop. It didn't come.

Now pin a time to it:

> The 6:40 bus showed up at 6:52. In between, I traced the cracks in the pavement with the toe of my shoe a dozen times.

With the time mentioned twice, the length of the wait is felt without a word about it. The word "frustrating" never appears.

## Common stumbling blocks

- **Using too many times.** If every sentence has a time, it turns into a log. One or two per scene is plenty.
- **Writing the time and stopping.** If you end on "It was three in the afternoon," only a number is left. Always follow it with what happened at that time.
- **Worrying about being exact.** In a story, it only has to feel right. "When the sun began to lean" is a perfectly good time.

## Tips for using draft()

After you talk for about thirty seconds, draft() offers two or three gentle questions. While you speak, stop once and ask yourself, "What time was it, roughly?" When a time comes to mind, the opening and the peak of the story often get clearer along with it.

## Try this today

Pick one short moment from today. Write its time in one line, then add one sentence about what that time did. Three lines are enough. Read them aloud once and check whether the time holds the scene in place.
