Most of us warm up when we start writing. What time we woke up, what the weather was like, where we went: we write it all in order. The writer needs that time, but for the reader it is time spent waiting for the story to begin. Cut the warm-up and start in the middle of the scene, and the piece gets lighter at once.

## What a warm-up paragraph is

It's the sentences that set things up before the story starts. "It was a Saturday morning," "I finally had some free time," "The weather was nice, so I decided to go out." None of it is wrong, but nothing has happened yet. A reader expects that by the first line, something is already going on.

## Three ways to find your starting point

1. **Cover the first paragraph with your hand.** If the piece still makes sense without it, that paragraph was a warm-up.
2. **Find the first "but."** Words like "but," "then" and "suddenly" often sit right where the real story begins.
3. **Find the first line where someone speaks or moves.** Start at the first bit of dialogue or action.

## Example: Saturday at the market

> It was a Saturday morning. I woke up earlier than usual and the weather was nice, so I decided to try the market. It was crowded when I got there. I stood in front of a fruit stall. The woman handed me a peach.

Cut the warm-up and start in the middle:

> The woman at the fruit stall pressed a peach into my hand. "Try it. It came in this morning." I hadn't come to buy anything, but I found myself opening my wallet.

"Saturday morning" is gone and you don't miss it. If it matters, it can come later in a single line.

## Hand back the essentials in one line

Starting late doesn't mean throwing the background away for good. Once the first few lines have passed, one short sentence can give the reader what they need. For example: "It was Saturday, and I'd only come out to look around." Say it once, plainly, and move on. Readers forgive an explanation that arrives late far more easily than a slow start.

After you add the background line, reread the first three sentences. If a stranger could answer "who is here, and what is happening?", you cut just the right amount.

## Common stumbling blocks

- **Cutting so much the reader gets lost.** Essentials like who and where should follow in a line or two.
- **Forcing a dramatic opening.** It doesn't have to be a big event. One peach is enough.
- **Clinging to the paragraph you cut.** Don't delete it; keep it in a separate place. It may belong in another piece.

## Tips for using draft()

Even when you talk for about thirty seconds, it's easy to start with a warm-up: "I woke up and..." Don't worry about it. As you answer the gentle questions that follow, you'll start to see where the story really comes alive. That spot is where the hook of your story belongs.

## Try this today

Delete the first paragraph of something you wrote recently and start reading from the second. If it got better, the first paragraph was a warm-up. Next time, begin with that second paragraph.
