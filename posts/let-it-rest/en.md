The moment you finish writing, two feelings often arrive together: it's perfect, or it's a mess. Both come from still being inside it. Right after writing, you read what you meant. After a night, you read what is actually on the page. Leaving it overnight isn't putting it off; it's a way of letting a version of you, standing at a distance, become the reader.

## Why a little distance helps

Just after you finish, the situation and the feeling are still fresh in your memory, so you fill the gaps in the writing without noticing. After a night, only the page is left, and words that were clear before can look as if something is missing. The excitement or the embarrassment has also cooled, so you look at the sentences instead of your mood.

## Three steps for resting and fixing

1. **Close it at a clear point and decide when to reopen it.** Write the last sentence, save it, and name a time: "tomorrow morning" or "after dinner." Decide before you close it, or you'll keep fiddling with it.
2. **When you reopen it, read it once without touching anything.** Read it as if someone else wrote it. Notice what catches your eye first. It can be a line you like or a line that confuses you.
3. **Change exactly one thing.** The one thing that bothered you most: a confusing sentence, a missing "who," a line that explains a feeling. Change that one, stop, and save.

## Example: a phone call from my sister

What you wrote the first night:

> My sister called about that thing again. We ended the call badly, and I kept thinking about it all evening.

The next day, two questions come up. Which thing? Badly, how? Don't try to fix both. Pick one, and replace "that thing" with what it actually was:

> My sister called about the old piano again. We ended the call badly, and I kept thinking about it all evening.

You changed only one thing, and the piece became a scene you can see. The other question can wait for another time.

## What to do with everything else you noticed

Once you have chosen the one thing to fix, write the rest below the piece, one line each. Notes like "ended badly how?" That note becomes tomorrow's one change, or material for the next piece. Writing it down takes away the pressure to fix everything now.

## Common stumbling blocks

- **Reopening it too soon.** Ten minutes later, you're still inside it. Let at least one sleep or a few hours go by.
- **Fixing everything at once.** You can't tell which change helped, and you tire of the piece quickly. Change one thing at a time.
- **Turning distance into giving up.** If you close it without naming a time to return, it tends to stay closed. Write the time down before you close it.

## Tips for using draft()

When you talk for about thirty seconds, a story comes out quickly. The quicker it came, the newer it looks after a night. Once the story exists, don't rush to fix it; tomorrow, ask just one question: "Which line made me hesitate?" Fixing only that line is enough.

## Try this today

Pick one thing you wrote or said today. A message or a note will do. Decide when you'll reopen it tomorrow and write that time down. Tomorrow, read it once, change one thing, and stop.
