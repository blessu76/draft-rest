Much of a day is spent waiting: for a kettle to boil, for a bus, for a reply that hasn't come. We usually skip these minutes when we tell a story, because nothing seems to happen. But a wait is where tension lives. Something is about to arrive, and the person cannot make it come faster. Writing a small wait can give a scene a quiet pull without any plot.

## Why a wait works

A wait puts a small want and a small delay side by side, and the reader feels the gap between them. You don't have to explain that the person is anxious or bored. The way they spend the minutes shows it.

## Three steps to write a small wait

1. **Name what is being waited for in one line.** "The kettle hadn't clicked off yet." Keep it ordinary.
2. **Show what the person does with the time.** Do they check the screen, wipe the same spot twice, count the ceiling tiles?
3. **Stop just before it arrives.** End on the last second of the wait, or one step after it, and leave the arrival itself out.

## Example: the late bus

Say you first wrote this:

> I waited a long time for the bus and felt impatient.

Now rewrite it as a scene:

> The bus was eight minutes late. I read the timetable twice, then the advertisement beside it, and tucked my hands under my arms. When the headlights finally turned the corner, I realized I had been leaning toward the road the whole time.

The word "impatient" never appears, and you can still feel it.

## Common stumbling blocks

- **Naming the feeling.** "I was anxious" closes the door. Let the small actions carry it.
- **Filling the wait with backstory.** Stay inside the minutes. A flashback lets the tension out.
- **Making the wait too long.** A few lines are enough. A short wait reads like a held breath.

## Tips for using draft()

After you talk for about thirty seconds, draft() offers two or three gentle questions. If you tell it about a moment you were waiting, a question about what your hands or eyes were doing can give you the line you need. Let the answer be a small action, not a summary.

## Try this today

Recall one wait from today, even a short one: an elevator, a loading bar, a kettle. Write what you waited for in one line and what you did with the time in two more. Stop before it arrives. Read it aloud and see whether the wait feels like a wait.
