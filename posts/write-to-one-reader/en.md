When you try to write for everyone, the words go vague. You add a little explanation for this reader and a little politeness for that one, and the piece ends up belonging to nobody. A short piece reads best when it is written to one person. Picking a single reader doesn't shrink your audience; it gives your sentences something to lean on.

## Why one reader helps

A crowd has no face, so you can't tell what needs explaining and what doesn't. One person has a face. You know what they already know, what would make them smile, and where they would lose interest. Those small judgments are what keep a sentence concrete.

## Three steps to write to one reader

1. **Pick a real person.** Choose someone you know: a friend, a sibling, or yourself a year ago. A real face works better than "readers in general."
2. **Answer what they would ask.** Imagine one question that person would ask while reading, such as "So what happened next?" Answer it in your very next sentence.
3. **Drop what they already know.** If they were there, skip the background. Spend your words on what only you can tell them.

## Example: telling a friend about a trip

Say you want to write about a short trip. Written for everyone, it might read like this:

> The trip was wonderful, with beautiful scenery and delicious food, and I recommend it to anyone.

Written to one friend who hates crowds, it changes:

> You would have liked the early train. Almost nobody was on it, so I got the window seat and ate my rice ball while the fog lifted off the fields.

The second version isn't longer or fancier. It simply knows who is listening, so it can choose a detail.

## If it feels too personal

Writing to one person doesn't mean only that person can read it. When you finish, you can leave out the name and keep the voice. Readers who aren't that person usually still follow, because specific writing is easier to picture than general writing. Leave out private details you wouldn't want to share, and change names if you need to.

## Common stumbling blocks

- **Choosing a reader who is too vague.** "Young people" or "busy adults" is still a crowd. Pick someone you could call by name.
- **Explaining too much.** If your friend already knows the place, you don't need to describe it from scratch.
- **Sliding back into a speech.** If your sentences start sounding like announcements, reread the last line as if you were saying it to that person's face.

## Tips for using draft()

When you talk for about thirty seconds, it's natural to talk to someone. Imagine one listener as you answer draft()'s two or three gentle questions, and your answers will already sound like a person speaking. Shaping them into a story comes after.

## Try this today

Pick one real person and one thing that happened today. Write three sentences to them, including one detail only they would care about. Then read it the way that person would, and see whether it holds.
