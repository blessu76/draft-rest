Have you ever answered "I'm fine" and felt a different sentence sitting right behind it? Many moments worth writing about have a line like that, something you almost said and then swallowed. The unsaid line is often the most honest sentence of the day, and it can give a short piece its center. You don't have to say it to anyone. You only have to write it down once.

## Why the unsaid line is worth writing

What we say out loud is usually polished for the room. What we leave out shows what was at stake. When a piece holds both the spoken line and the unspoken one, a reader feels the gap between them, and that gap does the work a paragraph of explanation would otherwise try to do. It also keeps you from labeling your own feelings, because the unsaid line is something you could actually have said.

## Three steps to find it

1. **Pick one short exchange.** A chat at the door, a text you typed and deleted, a pause during a phone call. Choose a moment that lasted a minute or less.
2. **Write what was said, then what wasn't.** First write the spoken words as exactly as you remember them. Underneath, write the sentence you held back, in the words you would use with someone you trust. Don't soften it.
3. **Add what you did instead.** One small action fills the space where the sentence would have gone: you straightened a cup, looked at your phone, said "anyway." Put that action between the two lines.

## Example: "How's the new job?"

Say someone asks about your new job over coffee.

> "How's the new job?"
> "It's fine. Busy."

The line you didn't say:

> I'm not sure I'm good at this, and I'm afraid everyone will notice.

Now add the small action, and the moment becomes a scene:

> "It's fine. Busy." I turned the cup a quarter turn on its saucer. I had been planning to say something else since the stairs.

Notice that nothing here explains the feeling. The cup and the unsaid line carry it, and the reader fills in the rest.

## Common stumbling blocks

- **Turning the unsaid line into a speech.** Once it grows past two sentences, it has become an essay. Keep only the one line that would have been hardest to say.
- **Making the other person the villain.** The point is not to win the conversation afterward. Write what you held back, not a rebuttal to what was said to you.
- **Writing a line you wish you had said.** A clever comeback is not the same as an honest one. If the line makes you look good, it is probably not the one you held back.
- **Skipping the action.** Without the small thing you did instead, the unsaid line floats in the air. A gesture pins it to the moment.

## Tips for using draft()

draft() takes about thirty seconds of talking, adds two or three gentle questions, and shapes your answers into a story with a hook, a rising part, a peak, a reflection and a close. When one of the questions makes you hesitate, notice what you almost answered. That hesitation often points to the line you didn't say, and your next thirty seconds of talking is a good place to say it out loud.

## Try this today

Think of one exchange from today where you said less than you meant. Write the spoken line, then the unsaid one, then one small thing you did instead. Three short lines are enough. Read them aloud once, then decide whether the piece should keep all three, or keep only the spoken line and the action and let the reader guess the rest.
