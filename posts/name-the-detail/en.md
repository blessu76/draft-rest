Words like "happy," "upset" and "lonely" are easy to write. But a reader can't picture anything from them. Swap the feeling's name for one concrete detail, and the reader feels it without being told.

## Why a feeling's name is weak

A feeling's name is a conclusion. Someone handed only the conclusion never walks the road that leads to it. A detail is the scenery along that road. When there is one thing the reader can see or touch, they work out the feeling themselves. The same "hurt" lands harder through your detail because it meets the reader's own memory, and it stays with them longer.

## Three questions for picking a detail

1. **What could you see?** Cold coffee, a TV left on, a door open halfway: whatever caught your eye.
2. **What was your body doing?** Washing your hands again and again, turning your phone face down for no reason, pulling a chair out and pushing it back in.
3. **What did you hear?** The fridge humming, footsteps in the hallway, a room that suddenly went quiet.

You only need the first answer that comes to mind.

## Example: the walk home

> I felt left out because my sister hadn't texted me on the way home.

With a detail:

> On the way home I checked my phone over and over. Nothing from my sister. At the front door I looked one last time, then set it face down on the shoe shelf.

"Left out" never appears, yet the last gesture shows it. Turning the phone over says the feeling for you.

## Where to put the detail

Place it close to the moment the feeling was strongest. At the end of a paragraph, it lets the reader pause and sit with the feeling; in the middle, it brushes past naturally as the story moves. Either way, don't follow it with a long explanation. Let the detail speak and step back.

## Common stumbling blocks

- **Piling on details.** One is enough. A list of them means none is remembered.
- **Naming the feeling again after the detail.** "I put the phone face down. I felt hurt." makes the words redo what the detail already did. Trust it.
- **Hunting for a striking detail.** Ordinary objects and gestures often read as more real.

## Tips for using draft()

When you talk for about thirty seconds, it's easy to end on a feeling's name: "I was just upset." When the gentle questions come, try answering with one thing you saw or heard in that moment instead of the feeling's name. That one line is the best material draft() can work with when it shapes your story.

## Try this today

Find a sentence in something you wrote recently that names a feeling. Remember one thing you could see when you felt it, and replace the feeling's name with that sentence.
