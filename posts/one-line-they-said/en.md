Compare "Mom said she had been worried" with the line your mother actually said, and it's clear which one carries her voice. When you summarize what someone said, the content stays but the voice goes. Keep one memorable line exactly as it was said, and that person stands in the scene.

## Why one line is so strong

Everyone has their own way of speaking: rhythm, word choice, habits. The same meaning sounds completely different depending on who says it. A summary flattens that difference; the words as they were said bring it along. The reader doesn't need to be told who this person is.

## Three ways to choose a line to keep

1. **A short line.** One line is enough. A brief remark stays longer than a long stretch of dialogue.
2. **A line that sounds like them.** Keep the expression only they would use, the verbal habit, even the awkward word order.
3. **A line that doesn't match the feeling.** Someone worried who only asks, "Did you eat?" There is distance between what is said and what is meant.

## Example: the front door, late at night

> I came home late, and Mom said she had been worried.

Keeping the line:

> The hallway light was on. Mom didn't turn around. "I warmed up your dinner. Eat and go to bed."

"Worried" appears nowhere, but the light left on and the warmed-up dinner carry it. One short line shows exactly how this person expresses love.

## What to put before and after the line

A short note before the line about what the person was doing, and a short note after about what you did, is enough. The action before sets the temperature of the words; the reaction after shows where they landed. In the example, adding just one line after "Eat and go to bed" where you take off your shoes and sit at the table without a word makes the scene much deeper.

## Common stumbling blocks

- **Too many quotation marks in one scene.** One or two lines is plenty. The more there are, the weaker each one gets.
- **Inventing words you can't remember.** If you can't recall the exact sentence, it's more honest to give the gist indirectly.
- **Explaining the line afterwards.** The moment you write "that meant she was worried," the power drains out. Let the line do its work.

When you write down a real person's words, naming them by relationship, such as "Mom" or "the coworker next to me," instead of by name also protects their privacy.

## Tips for using draft()

When you talk for about thirty seconds, someone's words tend to come out on their own: "And then she said..." Don't summarize them; say the line the way you remember it. When you answer the gentle questions that follow, going back to that line helps you hold on to the part of the story where the sound is clearest.

## Try this today

Write down one line someone said to you today that stayed with you. It doesn't have to be exact. Add one sentence about what that person was doing when they said it, and you have a small scene.
