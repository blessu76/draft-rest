Some scenes have good details and still read flat. What's missing is usually a turn: a moment where something shifted, even slightly. A turn doesn't have to be dramatic. A mood changing, a small decision, or noticing something you hadn't is enough.

## What a turn is

A turn is the line between "before" and "after" inside one scene. Before it, you felt one way or expected one thing; after it, something was different. Without a turn a scene is a list. With one, it's a story.

## Three small turns to look for

1. **A feeling flipped.** Annoyed, then amused. Tense, then relieved.
2. **A small decision.** You stayed, you made the call, you said yes.
3. **A thing noticed.** Something you had walked past for years suddenly caught your eye.

## Example: waiting for the bus

> I waited twenty minutes for the bus. It was cold. The bus came and I got on.

With a turn:

> I'd been waiting twenty minutes in the cold and was getting properly annoyed. Then the woman standing next to me held out a spare pair of gloves. "You look cold," she said. By the time the bus came, I had stopped minding the wait.

The wait is the same, but one pair of gloves splits it into before and after.

## Common stumbling blocks

- **Making the turn too big.** "Everything changed" is hard to believe. A small shift reads as more honest.
- **Explaining the turn.** Instead of "that's when I realized," show the words or action where it happened.
- **Forcing a turn that isn't there.** Some moments are just moments. Choose another scene.

## Tips for using draft()

When you talk for about thirty seconds, words like "but," "then" and "suddenly" tend to show up. They usually sit right next to the turn. Use the gentle questions that follow to stay with that part a little longer.

## Try this today

Pick one scene from today and write it in two sentences: how it felt before, and how it felt after. Whatever sits between those two sentences is your turn.
