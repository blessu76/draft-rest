Write "the house was a mess" and the reader pictures a mess on their own. Write "the pill bottle with no cap had sat on the table for three days" and the reader sees how long people have been living there. One object is tiny, yet it can hold the mood of a scene and a person's circumstances all at once. Look around the room and write down the one thing your eye keeps landing on.

## Why one object can speak for the scene

Objects carry the marks of use. A notebook with worn corners, one slipper left by the door, a cup half drunk: each shows how someone has been getting by without any explanation. The reader notices those marks, guesses at the situation, and gets pulled into the story. Explain less, place an object, and the scene starts talking.

## Three steps to open a scene with an object

1. **Pick one object that caught your eye.** Anything your eyes kept returning to in that scene will do. It doesn't have to look important.
2. **Write its look and condition in one line.** Choose two or three of: color, size, where it sits, where it is worn. For example: "a blue mug with one handle broken off."
3. **Link it to a person or a moment.** In one sentence, say who picked it up or why it was left there.

## Example: the umbrella by the door

Say this is what happened today:

> A friend came over. We talked for a long time.

Now put an object in it:

> After my friend left, a folded umbrella stayed by the front door. The rain had stopped by lunchtime, but the umbrella never went home.

The fact that the umbrella stayed hints at "I think there will be another visit." Not a word explains a feeling.

## Common stumbling blocks

- **Listing many objects at once.** A list scatters the scene. One per scene is plenty, two at most.
- **Describing the look and stopping.** If you end on "There was a blue mug," it reads like a photo caption. Always tie it to a person or a moment.
- **Explaining what it means.** You don't need to write "that mug was our memory." Leave the reader room to notice.

## Tips for using draft()

After you talk for about thirty seconds, draft() offers two or three gentle questions. While you speak, stop once and ask yourself, "What object caught my eye then?" When an object comes to mind, the mood of the scene and the peak of the story often get clearer along with it.

## Try this today

Pick one object from where you are sitting right now. Write its look in one line, then link it to a person or a moment in one sentence. Three lines are enough. Read them aloud once and check whether the object carries the mood of the scene.
