Before a reading, a daughter tells her father, “Sit here.” She needs help folding the programs. Later, when he comes into the hall, he heads for the back row. Standing beside the lectern, his daughter taps the back of an empty chair in the front row. “Sit here.” This time she is inviting him to sit close enough to hear her voice.

## Why the same words can land differently

The reader hears the second line with the first still in mind. A change of place or task can give ordinary words a new meaning. Let the first line do its job in the moment. When it returns, show what changed. Even the row a chair is in can be enough.

## Three steps for using a line twice

1. **Choose something a person would say in the moment.** Start with a short line like “Sit here,” which can simply tell someone where to sit.
2. **Change the situation between the two lines.** The same person might speak from a new place, or the listener might say the line back. Give whoever says it the second time a fresh reason to speak.
3. **Keep the words and revise what surrounds them.** Just before the line returns, show the change with a visible action, like a hand tapping a chair.

## The line that returns at a reading

> She folded a program in half and ran a fingernail along the crease. “Sit here.” She pulled a folding chair out beside the table with her foot. He sat down and folded the programs that were left.
>
> When the doors opened, he headed for the back row. She set her pages down beside the lectern. One chair in the front row was empty. She tapped its back. “Sit here.”

The first chair is a place to share the work. By the time she puts down her pages and taps the second chair, his task has changed. He can sit close and listen.

## What to look for when you revise

Read the first line on its own. Trim any early hints about its later meaning and make its purpose in the first moment clear. Read the second line with the sentence before it. If the moments feel too alike, change something visible, like where a person stands, before emphasizing their tone.

## Tips for using draft()

After you speak for about thirty seconds, draft() offers two or three gentle questions. When you tell a story in which the same words came up twice, picture where the person was the second time. Saying what changed just before those words can give you a detail to set the two moments apart.

## Try this today

Write two lines about where you first heard a short phrase today. Write two more about when it might come up again. Use the same words both times. Read aloud and listen for whose place or task has changed.
